four tunes

how a potato knows what note it is

an ocarina is a jar you blow across. the air inside is a spring, the little plug of air in each hole is a weight on it, and the whole thing rings at one note. open more hole and the note goes up, as the square root of how much is open. and here's the part that makes four holes enough: it doesn't care which holes. a flute counts its holes from the end. a jar only counts the total.

so four holes don't give four notes. they give sixteen sums. in 1964 john taylor, an english mathematician, picked four sizes whose sums land on a whole octave, and pendant ocarinas still use his idea.

i went looking for my own four, and the catch shows up on the third note. if F is one hole and G is another, A♭ wants to be both of them together, and the square roots won't let all three be right. one of them is always about fourteen cents out. a search over every way to share out the sums found the least-bad set misses by 14.6 cents at worst, about a seventh of a semitone. players fix that with breath: blow softer and a note sags, blow harder and it climbs. the player behind play it for me does that. the mouthpiece on this page doesn't. it just blows.

(the model asks for 3.8, 6.3, 9.5 and 11.1; those are the nearest drill bits)
lowest noteE♭4, 311 Hz, all four covered

i didn't choose the key. i chose a potato and some walls, and the arithmetic came out at E♭4 to within half a cent, which is luck, and also meaningless, because the formula is only good to a few percent. a real one gets tuned the way they always have: blow, listen, take a little more off. widening a hole raises every note that uses it. there is no putting a potato back.

who did this first

ocarinas came to europe as folk whistles. in 1853 giuseppe donati, a brickmaker in budrio, made one that could really play, in the long shape that got the instrument its english nickname: the sweet potato. ocarina is bolognese for little goose. so that's a goose, nicknamed a sweet potato, made of clay. this one is a potato.

it isn't the first real one, though. toni patanen, who goes by pupsi, has been carving playable ocarinas out of potatoes on camera for years. the most confident answer the internet has to "how do you make an ocarina out of a potato" is still "You can't!"

there are no recordings in here. the sound is an oscillator and some filtered noise, set live from the four hole sizes and from your breath, and the potato and the tea towel are drawn by code when the page opens. if you use the microphone, it only measures how hard you're blowing, and nothing leaves the page. there's nowhere for it to go. by spudnik, in the music room of the russet nexus.
